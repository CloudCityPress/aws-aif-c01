# Introduction

Artificial intelligence has shifted from a research curiosity to a line item in nearly every business plan. Small retailers run AI for inventory forecasting, global enterprises use machine learning to optimize supply chains, and customer-service teams everywhere have replaced their first-tier scripts with AI assistants that learn from each interaction. The pace of adoption is the story; the gap between organizations that have an AI strategy and those that do not is widening every quarter.

Customer service is the most visible example. AI assistants now handle the long tail of routine questions, escalate the rest, and feed every conversation back into a continuous-improvement loop. The outcome for the business is faster resolution, lower per-contact cost, and a richer view of what customers actually want.

In healthcare, machine learning is changing diagnosis and treatment planning. A 2024 study from Harvard Medical School found that an AI tool called CHIEF could detect cancer with 94% accuracy across 19 cancer types and outperformed earlier methods on tasks ranging from molecular profile prediction to patient survival forecasting.[^005001] The point is not that the technology replaces clinicians. The point is that it lets clinicians make better-informed decisions faster.

The financial sector has embraced AI despite its conservative reputation. Fraud-detection systems built on machine learning analyze millions of transactions in real time and flag patterns that no human reviewer would catch. Industry analyses report institutions cutting fraud-detection time by 70% or more after deploying machine-learning models alongside their existing rules.[^005002] The savings are large, but the bigger win is customer trust.

The market reflects this. Grand View Research projects the global AI market at roughly $1.81 trillion by 2030, growing at a 36.6 percent compound annual growth rate from 2024.[^005003] Behind that number is a steady migration from custom-built models to managed AI services that take care of the infrastructure, the model hosting, and the security plumbing.

Amazon Web Services has been at the center of that migration. From pre-trained services like Amazon Rekognition for image and video analysis to platforms like Amazon SageMaker AI and Amazon Bedrock, AWS lets organizations of all sizes start with AI without the capital investment that an in-house team used to require.[^005004] In 2025 and 2026 the AWS AI portfolio expanded again with Amazon Bedrock AgentCore for production agent runtimes, Strands Agents as an open-source SDK for building agents, Kiro as an AI-powered software-development environment, and Amazon Quick as the unified business-intelligence and assistant experience for the workforce. The exam you are about to study reflects all of these additions.

Consider Lyft, the ride-share platform. Lyft uses Anthropic's Claude through Amazon Bedrock to power an AI agent that answers driver and rider questions and resolves issues without escalating most cases to a human. The agent has cut average resolution time by 87%.[^005005] At Toyota Motor North America, an Amazon Bedrock-based dealer assistant uses retrieval-augmented generation against official vehicle information to handle more than 7,000 dealer interactions per month, putting precise model-specific answers in front of dealership staff in seconds rather than minutes.[^005007] At Cox Automotive, five agentic AI products built on Amazon Bedrock AgentCore and Strands Agents now handle after-hours sales and service conversations end to end without human oversight.[^005008] These are not pilots; they are production deployments running today on the same services this book covers.

Generative AI, which broke into the mainstream in late 2022, has already changed how content gets produced and how software gets written. Marketing teams generate personalized content at scale; engineering teams collaborate with AI assistants that write tests, suggest refactors, and chase down bugs. Inside AWS, the same shift is visible: agentic systems now plan multi-step tasks, call business APIs through standard protocols such as the Model Context Protocol, and use managed memory services to remember the context of long-running interactions.

Important challenges come with the new capabilities. As AI systems grow more influential, questions about ethics, bias, accountability, and data leakage move from compliance edge cases to top-of-mind concerns. Companies that pair their AI investment with strong governance, clear data-handling policies, and ongoing workforce reskilling are the ones that compound the gains.

The convergence of AI with adjacent technologies is the next interesting frontier. Combining AI with **Internet of Things (IoT)** sensors creates "smart" ecosystems, from cities optimizing traffic flow in real time to plants predicting equipment failures days ahead. AWS supports this trend with services such as **AWS IoT Core** for secure device connectivity and **AWS IoT Greengrass** for running AI models at the edge.[^005006] These services are out of scope for the AI Practitioner exam, but they are useful context for thinking about where AI will be deployed next.

A natural reaction to the pace of change is a mix of excitement and apprehension. Both are reasonable. The most useful framing, in this author's view, is that AI is still a tool. Its value comes from amplifying human judgement, not replacing it. The professionals who learn how to scope an AI project, choose the right service for it, and manage the risks responsibly will be the ones who lead the next wave of business transformation.

For business professionals, AI literacy is now part of the job description. Whether you are personalizing customer experiences in marketing, improving risk models in finance, or optimizing operations on the production floor, AI will keep showing up in your work. The **AWS Certified AI Practitioner** exam (V1.1) prepares you to navigate that landscape with confidence.

Throughout this guide, we will not just cover AI technologies. We will also cover their practical business application. We will demystify the concepts that hide behind acronyms, walk through real-world use cases, and give you the vocabulary to make informed decisions about which AWS service belongs in which project.

The goal is not to turn you into an AI engineer or a data scientist. It is to make you a business professional who can spot AI opportunities, communicate effectively with technical teams, and make sound calls about whether and how to implement AI for a given problem. You will end the book with a clear view of what AI on AWS can do today, what it cannot do, and how to apply it responsibly.

The world of AI is moving quickly, and the surface area of useful knowledge keeps growing. As you work through this guide, you will gain the foundation to navigate that surface confidently and unlock new opportunities for yourself and your organization. Welcome.
